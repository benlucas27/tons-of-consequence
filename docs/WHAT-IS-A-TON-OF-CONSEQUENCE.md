# What is a ton of consequence?

A ton of consequence is a Test hundred of consequence. The side needed it. It helped them win the match or avoid losing it.

There are good tons, but not every ton is a ton of consequence.

## The laws (a ton of consequence is shortened to "toc")

Law 1 is the only hard law. Laws 2 to 13 add points, and law 8 takes points away. A hundred needs 45 points to count, and that bar is deliberately high.

1. **A toc wins or saves a match.** You can't score a toc in a loss or a dead rubber. This is the only hard law.
   - A dead rubber counts only if his side had already won the series and also won the match.
   - A draw counts only if his side was in danger of losing, or he batted a long, slow grind (under 45 runs per 100 balls, 250+ balls) to save it.
2. **A toc considers the scoreboard when the batter came in, except for openers.** Walking in at 3 for 80 matters. Walking in at 1 for 300 doesn't. Openers always come in at 0 for 0.
3. **A toc carries the innings.** What share of the team's runs did he score? Was he the only hundred? How far clear was he of the other batters? Did the team collapse around him? If the other ten batters added under 150 between them, it earns extra. If they added 450 or more, it loses a little.
4. **A toc is part of a winning chase or a close finish.** It doesn't have to finish the chase. It can be what made it possible.
5. **A toc impacts a series.** Deciders and undecided series count more than dead rubbers.
6. **Pace matters, but not always.** A fast hundred can change a game. A slow grind can save one.
7. **The moment matters.** A tribute, a farewell, a comeback, an emotional match, Boxing Day or New Year.
8. **It's hard to score a toc if there's no pressure on when you come in.** If his side led by more than 200 when his innings began, it loses points.
9. **The opening platform, the big stand (new).** An opener whose first-wicket stand set up a win counts, even if the match later looks out of reach. That is the Hayden and Langer case. We look at the size of the stand and when the second opener fell, and only count it when his side won.
10. **The late stand (new).** A hundred built in a partnership at the 7th wicket or later earns extra: most for 100+ runs, less for 60+.
11. **The lower order (new).** A hundred from number 7 or lower earns extra: a little from number 7, more from number 8 and below.
12. **An elite attack (new).** Only a truly elite attack counts: the three most-used bowlers averaging under about 25 (under 27 for a smaller reward), from each bowler's career record in our data. Ordinary strong attacks earn nothing. It works for 2006 on.

13. **Who it was scored against (new).** Tiers: 1 England, Australia and India; 2 New Zealand and South Africa; 3 Sri Lanka, West Indies and Pakistan; 4 everyone else. A hundred against a tier 4 side loses 10 points and against a tier 3 side loses 3. A side counts a tier higher in an era when it was stronger: West Indies up to 2000 and Sri Lanka from 1996 to 2010.

## How firm a verdict is

- Your own hand rulings stand, with one exception made on 16 Oct 2026: once the 12 laws were agreed, the laws' answer replaced your earlier ruling on the 164 hundreds where they disagreed. Bradman's are untouched.
- If a verdict flips when the weights are nudged, it's marked marginal. Full-evidence hundreds keep their current verdict. Partial-evidence ones get no verdict until reviewed.
- Every verdict shows the level of evidence behind it.
- **Articles that call an innings one of the greats** are linked beside the verdict on the site and help hand rulings. They set the evidence, not the points. Two are in so far (Laxman 281 at Kolkata and Stokes 135* at Headingley, both from Wisden). More come from Wisden, Wikipedia, Cricket Country, Scroll and cricket.com.au.
- Night watchmen and pre-2006 hundreds are judged by hand from match reports.

## Honest limits

- It is a first pass, not a measurement. The points agreed with 151 of your 258 earlier ball-by-ball rulings (59%); the other 107 now follow the rules.
- The new laws (9 to 11) did not lift that agreement, and adding a "very big score" law lifted it by only 3 rulings while pushing the count of tons up to about 700, so I left it out.
- About 649 hundreds clear the bar today.
- The partnership and opening-platform laws only work for hundreds with a ball-by-ball record. Older ones use hand rulings.

## For when someone asks "why is this a ton?"

> It had to help win or save the match. Then it scores for how much trouble his side was in, how much of the innings he made, whether he helped win a chase, what the series rode on, the pace, the occasion, the stand he was in, and where he batted. It loses points if his side was already miles ahead.
